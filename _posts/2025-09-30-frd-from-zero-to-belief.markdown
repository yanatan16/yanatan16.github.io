When I started my first 100-miler, the 2018 High Lonesome 100, I didn't really believe I could complete it. My internal monologue constantly reminded me I was not strong enough. I stuffed the voice as deep as possible. But, at multiple times during the race, it would pop out and overwhelm its restraints. At mile 76, I collapsed onto the ground in a heap of self loathing. My crew never let me quit though, and finally, I reached the finish line. Tears streamed down my face. I never believed it was possible.

<!--break-->

Even with an unbelievable finish under my belt, nothing had really changed. I kept signing up for 100-milers, and I kept fighting the same fight again and again, and losing. I quit each one. Without a big crew supporting and believing in me, I was unable to complete the distance. Three years, three 100-milers, three DNFs. I considered never signing up for a 100-miler again. Perhaps it just wasn't my distance. That was a difficult, identity-challenging thought.

I decided to give it one more shot at the 2022 Teanaway Country 100, this time training harder than ever. My determination and fitness got me 96 miles without a single incident. With four miles and 3000 feet of descent left, the finish should have seemed within reach. Instead, I found myself sitting on a log bawling to my pacer. "I can't do it. I'll never get down this mountain." I could hear cheers from the finish line, but they felt a lifetime away. I did eventually limp down the mountain to finish that race, earning my second buckle. But the breakdown I had shook me. How could I feel the finish was impossible when I was so close to it? Why, in my peak physical condition, could I not complete a 100-miler without drama?

We've all heard the story of Roger Bannister, who in 1954, was the first person to break the 4-minute mark in the mile. His monumental achievement was replicated only a month later. Gordy Ainsleigh's running of the 1974 Tevis Cup paved the way for the first 77 racers of the Western States Endurance by 1977. Before Mark Williams came over from England to finish the Barkley Marathons in 1995, no one believed the course Lazarus Lake had created was even possible to finish within the time limit. It took 6 more years to master the even-harder course, but 5 runners followed in Mark Williams' footsteps by finishing between 2001 and 2004. These pioneering runners proved that the barriers they crossed were merely psychological. They provided a critical element to their peers: belief it was possible.

It wasn't the training I needed (although it did help). It wasn't lighter gear. It wasn't more course knowledge. It was belief: Belief that my body and mind were capable of completing any distance in front of me.

Belief is knowing that the journey is possible. When faced with the choice of continuing or not, it helps to know it's possible. Stepping out to do something possible is easier than stepping out to see if it is possible.

Belief is knowing that your body can handle the rigors. As runners, we rely on our bodies. They are the only vehicle we have. No team car waiting with a spare body. When discomfort or pain show up, it's easy to catastrophize and imagine it getting worse. Believing your body can handle it is how you can push through.

Belief keeps the anxiety at bay. Personally, I'm plagued by anxiety every day, and much more on race week. But, belief tempers the anxiety. With belief, we can achieve confidence.

Belief has changed the way I run. Since the 2022 run, I've completed 5 100-milers without ever coming close to quitting, despite some extreme conditions (see Utah 115, sand). I don't worry about going too slow at the beginning. I don't worry when I hit a low patch. I don't worry when I'm dehydrated and overheating. I believe my body and mind can weather the storm.

So if you're attempting something difficult, or having trouble completing a difficult challenge, remember to believe you can. It makes a world of difference.
