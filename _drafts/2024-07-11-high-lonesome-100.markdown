On Friday July 19, 2024, I ran the [High Lonesome 100](https://highlonesome100.com). It would be my fifth official 100-mile finish, second High Lonesome 100 finish, and second 100-mile finish in 6 weeks. I audibly recapped my experience here on Season 3 Episode 14 of my podcast, [Running With Problems](https://runningwithproblems.run).

<!--break-->

### Race Preparedness and Intentions

Coming into the race, I was very stressed. As a result, I went into the race without any pressure on myself for time or performance. I really didn't have space in my life for that. I also hadn't seen my wife in a week, and I wouldn't until Blanks Cabin Aid Station (Mile 83). The previous weekend, I had crewed and paced my friend Nathan at Hardrock, which involved very little sleep and a good 20 mile run, not the best taper week idea, but definitely something I didn't want to miss.

As Communications Director for the race, I was working up until the day before the race. But I was really excited to see the Comms Volunteers out on course and check in on them at every aid station. They all knew I was running and would be cheering me on.

Although, I had no crew and no pacers for the race, I expected to see a ton of friends out on course, at every aid station and in between. But, it did mean I had to rely completely on drop bags. Initially, I packed my divide gear for my Hancock drop bag, but this would have made me fail gear check at Cottonwood. I didn't realize it until an hour before the drop bags were getting picked up (Thanks David!). I had to reorganize the drop bags immediately (lol).

The weather predicted lots of rain, especially overnight while I would be on the high ridge of the Continental Divide Trail (CDT). I packed 3 different rain jackets (light to start, heavy in the middle, light for the second morning). My plan was to rely a lot on aid station food, with Maurten gels and whatever hydration mix the race had in between.

### Race Day

Before the race, I slept at the Start/Finish line in my car, which made the race morning very convenient. I woke up, got prepared, and went to gear check. It reminded me of Niwot's Challenge morning, when I always sleep in my car and need to gear check early. David Fox checked my gear, and we were both happy when I passed!

They turned on some music at the start line, and the first song to play was a country song with the lyrics: "You gotta find your people." This phrase would stay with me all day as my mantra.

#### Start - St Elmo 1 (Mile 25)

I started off near the back of the pack, running easy and chatting with people. I spoke with Mitch, who had volunteered with us for a few years. He would go on to DNF early unfortunately, but would be the first of many friendly faces I'd see and speak to out there. I was hiking the uphills, figuring if I probably wouldn't be running them in 50 miles, why run them now? Little did I know, I'd actually be running faster in 50 miles than I was now. We ran into Raspberry 1, and I stopped for a moment to get some water from my friend and fellow Speedlander, Shad Mika. I also checked in on the Comms volunteers, who cheered me on.

As we started the Little Browns climb up Antero, I was leading a small group of people, keeping good consistent pace. Eventually, I felt some pressure being in the front of the group and decided to drop behind the group and eat some gel. Getting up the first climb takes a very long time. Kevin Cannaday, who had just run Western States, passed me on the blimb. He was easy to see ahead in his bright yellow shirt. Eventually, I finally summited Little Browns, being rewarded with grand views.

As I summited the climb, I saw one of the photographers who had been to the race every year, Jordan Chapell, taking photos at the top. I said hi as he cheered me on and got some good photos.

I started down from the top and passed our newest Comms checkpoint Skyline. I walked up to the volunteers and thanked them for being here, high above treeline. I then started down the "switchbacks," a series of giant gravel switchbacks on this 4x4 jeep road, eventually descending 2,000 feet to treeline and reaching the first "real" aid station, Antero. I saw Caj, my friend from Boulder Trail Runners, Stephanie Gnoza, from Boulder's Rocky Mountain Runners, who always volunteers up at Antero, and David Hoff, Antero AS Captain, who has worked with the race every year he wasn't running it. The aid was out of gels, which was a bit disappointing, but I grabbed some refills and oranges, and started the long descent down Baldwin Gulch.

Baldwin Gulch is a secret difficulty of Hilo. It's a long downhill on a 4x4 road with tons of baby head rocks (rocks the size of baby heads). It has the effect of causing hot spots on your feet, which in 50 miles, will become blisters, and totally mess up the second half of your race. So you have to treat it with respect. When I got down to the bottom, I ran past our other new Comms Checkpoint, Baldwin. I said hi to the volunteers there, who encouraged me to not stop. I asked them how it was going, and they said initially the generator wouldn't start, but Caleb (the RD) had fixed it. Apparently an oil issue. I ran into the small town of Alpine and started a long 4-mile slightly uphill forest road to St Elmo.

The Alpine to St Elmo road was long. I threw in some tunes and tried to catch up to Kevin Cannaday, who was still just a few hundred yards ahead. Along the way, I got interviewed by someone with the livestream. I'm not sure if that ever made it on air. A few miles later, I did catch up to Kevin, only to learn that his rib pain was really affecting him. Kevin has finished every High Lonesome up til now, but unfortunately, he would DNF this one at Hancock. I ran ahead of him before we reached St Elmo. I was excited as I ran through the ghost town, but I forgot that there's a punchy climb before the aid station itself. I labored up it, now feeling the heat of the day, and strolled into St Elmo AS. I was greeted by Whitney, one of our Medical Directors, and Zach King (I think), filling in for our co-Course Director Kylie. I snagged some OJ at this aid and boy did that hit the spot! I wish more aid stations carried fruit juice. After some good aid time, I headed out to climb Laws Pass to Cottonwood AS.

#### St Elmo 1 (25) - Hancock (49)

Energized by St Elmo, I started out strong on the trail up to Laws Pass, but was soon beat back down by the heat and climbing. I chose to take it relatively easy so as not to push my heart rate so high so early in the race. I knew the out and back over Laws Pass would be tough, no need to make it tougher. I ran mostly alone on this section. The front runners started passing me on their way back from Cottonwood, and I cheered them on. I recorded a video log for Miranda, claiming I only had 55 miles to go to see her! Down from 83 at the start. I told her I liked the new additions to her playlist, filled with 90's alternative and modern indie rock.

When I saw the last few switchbacks to the top, it felt like such a relief. Got a summit photo from Justin, and started down the other side. My downhill legs were OK, but my energy was pretty depleted. A hot spot was forming on the bottom of my left food, center under the ball of my foot, started by a slip on some rocks on Baldwin Gulch. I had a sock change waiting for me in my drop bag at Cottonwood, just had to make it there to take care of my feet.

The northern side of Laws is steeper, and therefore, more fun to descend. But also, steeper to ascend. I got to see many friends on their way up as I came down. Liz Canty, who I met after the race, said "double Laws is a bitch," which is a statement I can get behind. Saw Jana running in 4th place! So stoked for her. Saw Silke (podcast guest!) looking strong. I saw Christy Amero (friend from Boulder and she was on our podcast How to Prepare for Your First 100 Miler) about 1 mile out from the aid station, making her 2 miles ahead of me; less than I'd thought she'd be after how slow my first 50K has gone. I also saw Courtney Boyle (also a former podcast guest, check her episode out!) around the same point, which is farther ahead than I'd thought she'd be. David Hettena (Hilo friend from Salida) was running strong uphill, and Kody Riley (fellow Speedlander) was too. Jon Davis had his usual dead-in-the-eyes look, but this was very understandable for someone on their second mountain 100 in a week. I'm sure there were more, but they start running together in my head.

Cottonwood AS was buzzing with activity in the small trailhead staging area. I was stoked to get here so I was running well on the way in. I saw Alden and gave him a hug. Marc Schwalbenberg (the AS Captain and a friend) came over to take care of me. I glanced over under a tent with drop bags in it and saw Caleb (the RD and a good friend) sitting on the grass and staring at his laptop. I said, "I'll sit next to that guy" and plopped right down next to him. It took him a moment to realize that it was me! Turns out, he's on the phone at that very moment with Kevin and Steve, the CommsHQ team.

Marc grabbed my drop bag, and I dumped it on the ground. I saw the bags full of my "Divide Gear." This meant I didn't fuck up my drop bags, and I would be able to pass gear check! I did a little jig dance. My biggest anxiety about the race was alleviated. I'd be allowed to continue. But, I still had things to do; I got to work on my feet. Strip shoes and socks, inspect hot spots (they looked OK), clean off feet, wipe down feet, lube up feet, put on new socks... Uh-oh! I accidentally packed two right feet socks (they're toe socks, so they only work on one foot). Whoopsie. I would have to reuse my current left sock; hopefully the hot spot wouldn't get worse. I put extra lube on and got them ready to go.

While sitting next to Caleb, I asked him how the race was going. He said it was going super smooth. I inquired about a couple issues I had heard about along the way, but he said of course there were a few issues, but overall, really well. This was my other big anxiety going into the race, wondering how the Comms team would do without me at CommsHQ. This made me feel so good to hear. I think this really helped me unlock my happiness that would propel me through the back half of the race.

I ate a nice amount of food, potatoes and watermelon with salt were especially good. Packed in a bunch more gels. I did the quickest gear check this side of the Mississippi and headed out!

The second climb up Laws was more enjoyable than the first. My headspace was really good after my conversation with Caleb and Marc taking such good care of me. Fresh lube on the feet felt good, and food had energized my pace. I saw Kevin on his way in and encouraged him. I got to chatting with Hillary Osborne, who I recognized but couldn't place in my memory at first. After some talking, I remembered that she had captained an aid station at Westline Winder (one of Freestone Endurance's 50Ks in September) the last couple years. We had some good conversation as we made our way up that climb. It started raining on us. I held off putting on my jacket for a switchback or two, but gave in. Two minutes later, it stopped. I stripped my jacket without stopping, leaving it hanging out the sides of my vest. It looks dorky, but it saves precious momentum.

I felt like the climbs up to 12,000 feet were really doing me in, so I figured Hillary would pass me and be gone, but in fact the opposite happened. I was still moving at a snail's pace up the final switchbacks, but I had started moving up in the world.

The descent back to St Elmo was reasonably unremarkable. I remembered being here in 2018, also dying of heat and slowness. This is where I said to myself, "if I'm gonna DNF this race, I'm gonna do it having fun." And I started bombing the descent. This year however, I felt no such need to bomb. I was having fun already and ran at a good pace across and down the valley back to St Elmo.

I reflected on my mantra of the day, "Find Your People." I had been saying hi to friends for the last few hours, one after the other. It really felt like I was among my people, out here in the woods, running for no particular reason other than because we can.

St Elmo 2 was disappointing only because they were now out of Orange Juice, which I had been looking forward to. I had some pickle juice and that perked me right up. The volunteers were so nice. I think Jesse Miller was there, cheering, but also taunting me. I didn't stay long. I took a couple perogis and quesadillas in a foil pouch to go, and I started walking.

The next 5 miles were on the road to Tin Cup Pass. Exposed, slightly uphill, and rocky jeep road. The old Course Director Jon Clinthorn used to say this was a "good road for eating." I tried to take that idea to heart. I walked almost the entire section. At the beginning, it was hard to eat (with so much climbing up high in altitude and brutal heat), but after 20 minutes or so, I could eat more regularly. Eventually, I made it to Tin Cup AS, where Jon and Jennifer Denese welcomed me. Jennifer was so excited to see me and snagged a photo for the Discord. I grabbed a few more gels, drank some Ginger Ale, and kept it moving.

Onto the Continental Divide Trail (CDT), progress was coming a little quicker. The sun wasn't as high and I could feel the air getting cooler. I was recovered and could jog more. I happily ran to the next climb. About 43 miles in, I recorded a little video message for Miranda, noting I _only_ had 40 miles to go to see her. The views on these unnamed passes on the way to Alpine Tunnel were incredible as dusk neared. Memories of 2018 flooded in of me running super fast, feeling my second wind. This time around, I ran moderately but hoping I could keep it up for longer. I ran the last couple miles on the railroad grade into Hancock, coming in at around 9:15, almost an hour after my split from 2018.

In the dark, it might have been hard to see friendly faces, but Hancock was lit super well by all sorts of lights. People were camped by the side of the trail everywhere. I scanned for someone I knew. I saw Sammie Lewis (of Golden Endurance; also a podcast guest). Gabe Dorn (also of Golden Endurance) was there too, dressed in a ridiculous green outfit. Not sure what the theme was. Erich (friend from Boulder) was working the aid too, and came to me as soon as he saw me. We walked to the chairs. I saw Jana; but I took a double take; she was running in 4th place; what was she doing here? Apparently she got a killer asthma attack and had to drop at Hancock. That must have been hours ago!

I sat down and dumped out my drop bag. I ordered broth immediately. One of my favorite things about ultras is downing a shit-ton of broth through the night. It always goes down well. I changed my shirt and put a thermal into my bag, worrying that bad storms overnight might bring the chills in. I had packed a little toiletry kit and quickly brushed my teeth, which amazed Erich. It felt so good to get that layer of sugar off my teeth.

Erich walked me out. He asked if I had a pacer. No pacer this time. He was impressed. It was now past 9:30, over 15 hours and 30 minutes into the race. I was feeling good. I didn't care it was late. I was happy it was nighttime and I was about to experience the beautiful divide section of High Lonesome.

#### Hancock (49) - Monarch (68)

I took the next mile relatively easy, climbing up the road part of the trail up to the base of Chalk Creek Pass. I saw a few runners ahead and started running to pass them. As I passed a lone runner, he said "the night would be hard for him." I decided to hang with him a minute to encourage him. He told me he was Siqi, a runner I had met at Niwot's Challenge earlier this year. It took me a moment to remember, so cool to see him again. I told him something Kevin had told me the day before, "if you just keep on walking, you'll get off the divide." It sounds simple and dumb advice, but I think it makes sense to a runner 50 miles in and heading into the night. For my money though, I was feeling good and started running and hiking strongly uphill.

I passed a couple more runners on the switchbacks up Chalk Creek Pass, then started running the downhills, passing a few more as the miles went by. Feeling good at night isn't new to me, but I was clearly in the zone. I ended up passing 13 runners on that segment.

Coming into Lost Wonder Hut, I saw Chris McBride, longtime captain of this aid station, marshaling the turn toward the aid station. I said hi and jogged the half mile to the aid station. I felt so good, but I didn't want to mess up this aid station. The next segment is the longest and hardest of the night segments. Lost Wonder Hut is setup so that you can do your aid on the porch of the cabin or go into the cabin to receive medical aid or warm up. I looked into the cabin and remembered being inside there in 2018, looking at every surface covered by bodies resting. In 2018, I said to my pacer, "We have to get out of here." It looked like a MASH unit. I didn't want to rehash that memory or get trapped inside the warm cabin. Instead, I sat on the porch for 2 minutes. I drank two cups of broth, refilled on gels, and asked for some SportShield wipes to relube some areas that needed relubing. Maggie (from Rocky Mountain Runners here in Boulder) said hi. She was waiting for her runner Chantal (also of RMR, but I hadn't met her), who I later learned was puking in the back. And just as soon as I was in, I was out. I briefly chatted with the Comms volunteer, making sure the internet was good. I walked the road back to the marshaled intersection, drinking my warm broth to go.

As I made the turn onto the road, I remembered the course change for this year, which eliminated a 200 foot pointless climb and extra mile of trail to keep us on this road. I thanked Caleb in my head for that change. I recorded another video log for Miranda, now bragging about feeling amazing at night. Only a marathon until I see you Miranda!

The road was dark. Night was fully set now. The trees and open road reflected back at me, keeping my pupils contracted and my brain awake. Two headlamps just turned off the road into the forest in front of me. My next runner to pass. It took only a few minutes to pass him.

The trail wound it's way through forest to reach Boss Lake, with moonlight reflecting off it's surface. Beautiful.

The climbing started. I passed Courtney Boyle. She was having some asthma issues. I stayed for a minute with her and encouraged her. Then I kept going.

As the climb was almost petering out, I felt the urgent need to poop. I knew we were about to hit treeline, and I wouldn't have a good private spot to go for at least two hours. I wandered off the trail, letting 3 people I just passed pass me back. But I had urgent business. I found a good spot and took off my pack. But then I couldn't dig here, the ground was too solid. I wandered deeper. People kept passing along the trail, which made me feel like I wasn't far enough off trail. Finally I found a place soft enough to dig. I dug my hole and did my business. Ok, now back to work. I saw someone's headlamp on the trail and walked to them. It was Courtney! But, where's my pack? I had left it somewhere in the woods. I started panicking and looking around with my headlamp. Courtney's pacer had a bright light and walked up to help me. I realized my headlamp could go super bright, so I clicked it up a notch and was able to spot the reflection of my pack in the distance, 20 meters up the trail. Thank god. Now Courtney's got a good story about me. lol.

I grabbed my stuff and settled myself. Time to get back to work. I said bye to Courtney and headed out into the night above treeline.
